## Summary

The first SYSTEMATIC AUDIT located by any cycle that measures a base rate of invalid /
fabricated citations in a large published corpus, and it separately reports SECURITY
venues. Added in cycle 12 against the base-rate open question on
institutional-incident-real-world-impact, which anecdotes cannot answer by construction.
Authors are from Nankai University and Tsinghua University; they release a verification
framework called CiteVerifier. IMPORTANT SCOPE LIMIT (see Limitations): the audited
population is peer-reviewed academic papers at security conferences, NOT threat-intel
reports published by agencies or vendors. It is a base rate for an adjacent population.

## Key claims

1. Corpus and headline rate: the authors analysed 2.2 million citations from 56,381 papers
   published at top-tier AI/ML and Security venues (2020-2025) and confirmed that 1.07% of
   papers contain at least one invalid or fabricated citation (604 papers), with an 80.9%
   increase in 2025 alone.
2. Security venues are not cleaner than AI venues, and the per-venue spread is wide.
   Reported comparison: "1.08% for AI venues vs. 1.01% for Security venues". Table 3
   per-venue invalid-paper rates: NDSS 2.56% (23 invalid citations / 18 papers), CCS 1.14%
   (20/20), USENIX 0.57% (12/11), S&P 0.56% (8/6); for AI/ML, NeurIPS 1.51% (391/308),
   ICML 0.93% (125/104), IJCAI 0.96% (53/51), AAAI 0.62% (106/86).
3. Model-level citation hallucination is orders of magnitude larger than the observed
   in-corpus rate: benchmarking 13 state-of-the-art LLMs on citation generation across 40
   research domains found ALL models hallucinate citations, at rates from 14.23% to 94.93%
   (14.23% DeepSeek, 94.93% Hunyuan), over 375,440 generated citations from 22,800 API
   interactions.
4. There is a documented human verification gap that explains how fabricated citations
   survive to publication: of 94 valid survey responses, 41.5% of researchers copy-paste
   BibTeX without checking and 44.4% choose no-action responses when encountering
   suspicious references, while 76.7% of reviewers do not thoroughly check references and
   80.0% never suspect fake citations.
5. Verification was three-stage and partly manual, which is why the rate is usable:
   automated flagging by CiteVerifier over all 56,381 papers, then manual review of all
   flagged citations by sixteen trained research assistants over ~one month with each
   citation independently checked at least twice, then a false-negative estimate sampling
   400 citations from the "valid" pool (no additional invalid citations found; invalid
   accuracy 98%, 392/400).

## Relevant quotes

- Abstract: "we analyze 2.2 million citations from 56,381 papers published at top-tier
  AI/ML and Security venues (2020–2025), confirming that 1.07% of papers contain invalid
  or fabricated citations (604 papers), with an 80.9% increase in 2025 alone."
- Abstract: "all models hallucinate citations at rates from 14.23% to 94.93%, with
  significant variation across research domains."
- Abstract: "76.7% of reviewers do not thoroughly check references and 80.0% never suspect
  fake citations."
- Definition used: "Invalid citations, including metadata error (incorrect metadata such
  as wrong title, authors, venue) and ghost citation (untraceable citations that cannot be
  found in any major academic database)".
- Stated limitation: "The detected invalid citations are likely to be a conservative lower
  bound on the true hallucination rate."

## Limitations of this source

- POPULATION MISMATCH, the single most important caveat for our topic: this audits
  peer-reviewed academic papers at security CONFERENCES. It does not audit CTI reports
  published by government agencies (ENISA) or commercial threat-intel vendors, which have
  no peer review, different citation practices (news articles and vendor blogs rather than
  indexed papers), and — per src-0004 — footnotes that are largely URLs. A ghost-citation
  detector keyed to "cannot be found in any major academic database" would not even be
  applicable to most CTI footnotes. Do NOT present 1.01% as the CTI base rate.
- The pipeline "verifies only title similarity", which the authors say makes the figure a
  conservative lower bound and means citations that are real-but-misused (the EY/ENISA
  failure mode of a live URL that does not support the claim) are largely out of scope.
- Rates are per-paper "contains at least one invalid citation", not per-citation, so they
  are not directly comparable to src-0004's 26-of-492 footnote figure, which is
  per-footnote. Comparing the two numbers directly would be an error.
- Read by automated fetch of the arXiv HTML rendering. The abstract and Table 3 rows were
  returned as quoted text and are recorded verbatim above; no PDF-level check was done.
