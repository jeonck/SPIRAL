## Summary

A SECOND named institutional incident of a published cyber-threat report containing
fabricated citations, independent of the ENISA case in src-0004. EY Canada's report
"Points of Attack: Uncovering Cyber Threats and Fraud in Loyalty Systems" (2025) was found
by GPTZero's investigations branch to have 16 of 27 references hallucinated; EY Canada
removed the study from its website. Added in cycle 12 as evidence of RECURRENCE across
institution types (EU agency, then Big Four commercial publisher) and, more sharply, as a
contrasting case of institutional RESPONSE: EY withdrew the report and issued a statement
referencing its AI commitments, whereas ENISA retained its reports and silently patched
the links (src-0009, src-0010).

## Key claims

1. GPTZero's investigations branch published a report on May 14 2026 finding that 16 of 27
   references in EY Canada's study "Points of Attack: Uncovering Cyber Threats and Fraud in
   Loyalty Systems" were hallucinated, and that 72% of the study was AI.
2. More than a half-dozen footnotes led to dead webpages or did not contain the cited
   information — the same discovery route as the ENISA case in src-0004 (external parties
   clicking through cited links), not internal QA.
3. Institutional response: following publication of the GPTZero report, EY Canada removed
   the study from its website and said it was reviewing why the study was published.
4. EY's public statement, as quoted by Going Concern (corroborating outlet, see below):
   "EY Canada takes the accuracy of all the content we publish seriously and we have an
   organisation-wide commitment to the responsible use of AI."

## Limitations of this source

- Secondary reporting on GPTZero's investigation, exactly the structural weakness src-0004
  has. The primary is https://gptzero.me/investigations/ey (dated MAY 14, 2026, by Om
  Ogale, Paul Esau and Alex Cui), which WAS fetched twice this cycle — but both automated
  fetches returned the scorecard widget in an unrun/default state reading "0 of 27
  references hallucinated", contradicting its own article body ("Almost all of the URLs are
  broken or fake, and more than half of the titles don't correspond to real sources") and
  contradicting two independent outlets that both report 16 of 27. The "0 of 27" is treated
  here as a fetch/rendering artefact of a JS scorecard, NOT as a competing claim, and no
  contradiction was opened on that basis. A future cycle with browser rendering should
  confirm 16/27 against the GPTZero page directly.
- The 16/27 and 72%-AI figures therefore rest on two secondary outlets (consulting.ca
  2026-05-19; Going Concern) reporting the same single upstream investigation. They are
  independent of each other but not of GPTZero.
- "72% of the study was AI" is an output of GPTZero's own commercial AI-detection product.
  AI-detector scores are contested as evidence and should not be treated as equivalent in
  reliability to the manually-checkable citation count. The citation count is the load
  bearing figure; the detector score is not.
- No direct quote from EY appears in this article; the "reviewing why the study was
  published" wording is the outlet's paraphrase. The verbatim EY statement above comes
  from Going Concern.
- Loyalty-systems fraud is cyber-threat/fraud content but is not threat-intelligence in
  the narrow IoC/TTP/attribution sense the rest of this knowledge base measures. It is a
  security-adjacent published report, and should be described that way.
