# Why this source is in the base

It is entered to answer the DESIGN question in `attribution-expressed-confidence-unmeasured`
open_questions[3] — "WHAT WOULD THE RIGHT MEASUREMENT EVEN LOOK LIKE?" — and NOT to supply any
measurement of any LLM. **It contains no LLM evaluation, no model, no number and no experiment.**
It is a normative best-practice document describing how HUMAN threat-intelligence analysts are
supposed to express confidence, and its value here is that it shows the measurement construct
this issue is looking for is already standardised and machine-readable in operational CTI
practice, even though no LLM evaluation in this base or found by cycle 49's search applies it.

> === Expressing confidence/estimative probability in an analysis
>
> NOTE: Expressing the confidence or the lack of it in an analysis is a critical step to help a
> partner or a third-party to check your hypotheses and conclusions.
>
> Analysis or reports are often shared together with technical details, but often lack the
> associated overall confidence level.
>
> To ascertain this confidence level you can use for example the MISP <<MISPTaxonomies>> called
> https://www.misp-project.org/taxonomies.html#_admiralty_scale[admiralty-scale] and/or
> https://www.misp-project.org/taxonomies.html#_estimative_language[estimative-language].
>
> This is a very human way to describe either globally an event or individual indicators of an
> event, with a set of easy to read human tags. (e.g: `admiralty-scale:source-reliability="a/b/c..."`,
> `estimative-language:likelihood-probability="almost-no-chance"`,
> `estimative-language:confidence-in-analytic-judgment="moderate"`).
>
> Generally it is good practice to do this globally for the event as this will enrich the
> trust/value if set. If it's a specific attribute, then the confidence can be described at more
> granular levels.
>
> Thus, adding confidence or estimative probability has multiple advantages such as:
>
> - Allow receiving organisations to filter, classify and score the information in an automated
>   way based on related tags
> - Information with low-confidence can still be shared and reach communities or organisations
>   interested in such information without impacting organisations filtering out by increased
>   confidence level
> - Support counter analyses and competitive analyses to validate hypotheses expressed in
>   original reporting
> - Expressing confidence allows the use of in the
>   https://www.misp-project.org/2019/09/12/Decaying-Of-Indicators.html/[decaying indicators feature]
>   in MISP to lower or increase the lifetime of an information
>
> Complement analysis with contrary evidences is also very welcome to ensure the original
> analysis and the hypotheses are properly evaluated.
>
> TIP: <<MISPTaxonomies>> contain an exhaustive list of confidence levels including words of
> https://www.misp-project.org/taxonomies.html#_estimative_language[estimative probability] or
> confidence in analytic judgment.
>
> TIP: threat-intelligence.eu includes an overview of the
> https://www.threat-intelligence.eu/methodologies/[methodologies and process to support threat
> intelligence].

# THE POINT FOR THIS ISSUE: THE STANDARD SEPARATES TWO AXES THAT THIS PROJECT HAS BEEN CONFLATING

The tag examples above are not three interchangeable ways of saying "how sure am I". They are
drawn from two taxonomies and they measure **three distinct things**:

1. `admiralty-scale:source-reliability` — how reliable the SOURCE of the information is.
2. `estimative-language:likelihood-probability` — how likely the claim is to be TRUE
   (Kent-style words of estimative probability, e.g. "almost-no-chance").
3. `estimative-language:confidence-in-analytic-judgment` — how much confidence the analyst has
   in the JUDGMENT ITSELF, i.e. in the sufficiency and quality of the evidence and reasoning
   behind it (e.g. "moderate").

**Axes 2 and 3 are separately tagged and can move independently.** An analyst may hold that an
attribution is probably true while having low confidence in the analytic judgment because the
evidence underdetermines it — and the standard gives that state a name and a machine-readable
encoding.

That distinction is exactly the one `attribution-expressed-confidence-unmeasured`
open_questions[3] arrived at independently, from the other direction, when it argued that the
well-posed target is not "is the model calibrated" but "whether a model's expressed confidence
DISCRIMINATES between reports whose attribution is determined by the evidence and reports whose
attribution is not". `confidence-in-analytic-judgment` IS that quantity, under an operational
name, and it is already distinguished from likelihood in the standard analysts actually use.

# LIMITS THAT MUST TRAVEL WITH THIS ENTRY

1. **This is a normative standard, not evidence about model behaviour.** It cannot support any
   claim about how any LLM behaves, and it must never be cited as a measurement.
2. **It is a community best-practice document, not a peer-reviewed publication** — a MISP-project
   repository file with no DOI, no venue and no stated author; the master branch was read with no
   commit pin, so a successor should re-pin it.
3. **The level sets themselves were NOT read.** This file only gives EXAMPLE tag values
   (`"a/b/c..."`, `"almost-no-chance"`, `"moderate"`). The full enumerations live in the MISP
   taxonomy pages it links to (`misp-project.org/taxonomies.html#_admiralty_scale` and
   `#_estimative_language`), which cycle 49 did NOT fetch. Anyone wanting to state how many
   levels each axis has, or what they are, must fetch those pages and add them properly.
4. `JP 2-0` is ABSENT from this file (checked by exact string). A web-search summary attributed a
   JP 2-0 reference to this document; that attribution is WRONG and is recorded here so nobody
   re-imports it.
5. The Admiralty scale's second axis — information credibility, the numeric 1–6 half that
   normally accompanies the A–F source-reliability half — is NOT named in this file. Only
   `source-reliability` appears. Do not assume the credibility half is present here.
