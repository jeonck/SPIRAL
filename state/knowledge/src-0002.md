## Summary

CTIBench (arXiv 2406.07599) is a benchmark suite built specifically for CTI-relevant
tasks, motivated by the authors' observation that general-purpose LLM benchmarks do not
cover practical CTI work. It bundles five sub-tasks and evaluates five LLMs
(ChatGPT-4/gpt-4-turbo, ChatGPT-3.5/gpt-3.5-turbo, Gemini-1.5, LLAMA3-70B, LLAMA3-8B)
against each. Performance varies sharply by task: models do reasonably on
knowledge-recall-style multiple choice (CTI-MCQ) and CVE-to-CWE root cause mapping
(CTI-RCM), but attack-technique extraction (CTI-ATE, mapping malware behavior to MITRE
ATT&CK techniques) is weak across the board, and threat-actor attribution correctness
lags well behind "plausible-sounding" attribution — a gap directly relevant to the risk
of confident-but-wrong CTI output.

## Key claims

1. Task difficulty is highly uneven: best model scores were CTI-MCQ 71.0% (GPT-4-turbo),
   CTI-RCM 72.0% (GPT-4-turbo), but CTI-ATE (MITRE ATT&CK technique extraction) F1 topped
   out at only 0.6388 (GPT-4-turbo), with weaker models scoring as low as 0.1562
   (LLAMA3-8B).
2. On CTI-TAA (threat actor attribution over 50 real reports), "plausible" attribution
   rates are much higher than "correct" attribution rates for every model tested (e.g.
   GPT-4-turbo: 86% plausible vs 52% correct; Gemini-1.5: 74% plausible vs 38% correct).
   This is a concrete, quantified instance of LLMs producing confident/plausible-sounding
   but factually wrong CTI attributions — directly relevant to hallucination risk in
   attribution use cases.
3. Model scale/quality matters but does not close the gap: even the strongest model
   (GPT-4-turbo) does not exceed ~72% on any task, and MITRE ATT&CK technique extraction
   in particular remains a hard, unsolved task for all five evaluated models.

## Relevant quotes / data

- "existing benchmarks provide general evaluations of LLMs, there are no benchmarks that
  address the practical and applied aspects of CTI-specific tasks" (paper's stated
  motivation).
- Reported scores table (from HTML rendering of arXiv 2406.07599v3):
  CTI-MCQ (accuracy): GPT-4-turbo 71.0%, GPT-3.5-turbo 54.1%, Gemini-1.5 65.4%,
  LLAMA3-70B 65.7%, LLAMA3-8B 61.3%.
  CTI-RCM (accuracy): GPT-4-turbo 72.0%, GPT-3.5-turbo 67.2%, Gemini-1.5 66.6%,
  LLAMA3-70B 65.9%, LLAMA3-8B 44.7%.
  CTI-ATE (F1): GPT-4-turbo 0.6388, GPT-3.5-turbo 0.3108, Gemini-1.5 0.4612,
  LLAMA3-70B 0.4720, LLAMA3-8B 0.1562.
  CTI-TAA (correct% / plausible%): GPT-4-turbo 52%/86%, GPT-3.5-turbo 44%/62%,
  Gemini-1.5 38%/74%, LLAMA3-70B 52%/80%, LLAMA3-8B 28%/36%.

## Limitations of this source

- Numbers were extracted via automated HTML-to-summary tooling from the arXiv HTML
  render, not manually cross-checked cell-by-cell against the PDF tables; treat as
  approximate pending manual verification.
- CTI-VSP (CVSS severity prediction) results were reported as Mean Absolute Deviation
  (lower is better) and are not directly comparable to the percentage/F1 metrics above;
  omitted from key claims to avoid a misleading comparison.
- Model list is from mid-2024-era releases (gpt-4-turbo, Gemini-1.5, LLAMA3); results
  may not generalize to newer model generations available as of 2026.

### FAIL — key claim 2's *interpretation* is not supported by the paper

**Correct and Plausible are NESTED, not disjoint.** Section 4.2, verbatim:

> "Based on these categories, we compute two types of accuracy: Correct Accuracy, which is the
> fraction of correct answers, and Plausible Accuracy, which is the fraction of correct and
> plausible answers combined."

Plausible Accuracy **contains** Correct Accuracy. So "the plausible rate is much higher than the
correct rate" is **true by construction** for every model and is not an empirical finding. The
empirical quantity is the **difference** — the plausible-but-not-correct share, derived by
subtraction and **not printed in the paper**: GPT-4-turbo **34**, GPT-3.5-turbo **18**,
Gemini-1.5 **36**, LLAMA3-70B **28**, LLAMA3-8B **8** percentage points.

**"Plausible" is not the paper's hallucination category.** Section 4.2, verbatim:

> "For the CTI-TAA task, we categorize the predictions into three types: correct answer (when the
> LLM accurately identifies the threat actor or one of its aliases), plausible answer (when the
> threat report lacks sufficient details to pinpoint the answer, but the LLM provides a plausible
> or related threat actor within a similar group), and incorrect answer (when the LLM misattributes
> the threat actor due to misjudgment, hallucination, or spurious correlation)."

"Plausible" is scoped to **underdetermined input** answered with a related actor from a similar
group; **misjudgment / hallucination / spurious correlation is the separate third bucket.** The
paper's own confident-but-wrong rate is therefore `100 − Plausible Accuracy` (derived, not
printed): GPT-4-turbo **14%**, GPT-3.5-turbo **38%**, Gemini-1.5 **26%**, LLAMA3-70B **20%**,
LLAMA3-8B **64%**.

Two further exact-string results: **`plausible-sounding` does NOT occur in this paper** (key
claim 2 and `index.json` present it in scare quotes as if quoting), and **no sentence in the
paper interprets the correct-versus-plausible gap** — the interpretation is entirely ours.

Judging procedure, verbatim: *"To further ensure accuracy, we manually verify each LLM response
to account for the multiple aliases that threat actors often use."* (§3.5) and, Appendix D,
*"we employed a graph-searching algorithm to determine the accuracy of model predictions."*

### What survives

CTIBench still supports a confident-but-wrong finding — the paper's *incorrect* bucket is
explicitly hallucination-inclusive and runs 14–64% — but **the number is not 86 vs 52, and the
gap is not evidence of overconfidence on its own.** Recorded as contradiction **`ctr-0002`**
against `attribution-confident-wrong-gap`. Nothing above this appendix has been deleted or
rewritten.
