## Summary

CyberThreat-Eval (arXiv 2603.09452, submitted 10 Mar 2026, cs.CR + cs.CL) is an
expert-annotated benchmark built from "the daily CTI workflow of a world-leading
company". Authors: Xiangsen Chen, Xuan Feng, Shuo Chen, Matthieu Maitre, Sudipto
Rakshit, Diana Duvieilh, Ashley Picone, Nan Tang; affiliations Microsoft Research,
Microsoft, and Hong Kong University of Science and Technology.

Why it was added (cycle 9, T3 on `ioc-extraction-reliability`): this is the FIRST
source in the knowledge base other than src-0003 that reports a numeric evaluation of
LLM-based IoC extraction from threat reports against human-expert ground truth, by a
team with no affiliation to PRISM/LANCE's authors. It was found by widening the search
away from "LANCE/PRISM replication" (which cycle 6 already established returns nothing)
to "any independent 2025-2026 evaluation of LLM IoC extraction with validated ground
truth". The paper does NOT cite LANCE or PRISM (arXiv 2506.11325) — its independence is
total rather than adversarial, which is the useful property here.

The benchmark spans the three-stage real analyst workflow the authors define — triage,
deep search, TI drafting — and deliberately avoids multiple-choice framing, which they
argue does not reflect what analysts actually do.

## Key claims

1. On IoC extraction from real-world CTI material, **vanilla LLMs reach precision
   0.8240 (GPT-4o) and 0.8503 (o3-mini)**; fine-tuning helps GPT-4o (0.8846) but a
   smaller fine-tuned model is much worse (GPT-4o-mini FT, 0.6944). Recall and F1 are
   NOT reported for this task, and the paper gives no reason for their absence. The
   evaluation covers 1310 IoCs.
2. On MITRE ATT&CK TTP identification in the same table and same study, performance is
   far lower still: precision 0.2787 / recall 0.2270 (GPT-4o), 0.3480 / 0.1759
   (o3-mini), 0.2387 / 0.1846 (GPT-4o FT), 0.1771 / 0.1414 (GPT-4o-mini FT) — i.e. a
   large within-study gap between mechanical IoC extraction and TTP mapping, measured
   by one team on one corpus with one model set.
3. Triage-stage priority scoring shows an extreme precision/recall asymmetry: recall
   (Accepted) 0.90-1.00 across all four models but precision (Accepted) only
   0.27-0.40 — the models accept nearly everything, so the triage stage as automated
   here would pass through roughly 2 of every 3 items that a human analyst rejects.
4. The authors' stated qualitative finding: "LLMs often lack the nuanced expertise
   required to handle complex details and struggle to distinguish between correct and
   incorrect information", and the workflow they describe therefore retains "both
   external ground-truth databases and human expert knowledge", with human experts
   iteratively providing feedback.

## Relevant quotes

Abstract (verbatim, confirmed identically from two independent fetches of the /abs and
/html pages):

> "Analyzing Open Source Intelligence (OSINT) from large volumes of data is critical
> for drafting and publishing comprehensive CTI reports. This process usually follows a
> three-stage workflow -- triage, deep search and TI drafting. While Large Language
> Models (LLMs) offer a promising route toward automation, existing benchmarks still
> have limitations. These benchmarks often consist of tasks that do not reflect
> real-world analyst workflows. For example, human analysts rarely receive tasks in the
> form of multiple-choice questions. Also, existing benchmarks often rely on
> model-centric metrics that emphasize lexical overlap rather than actionable, detailed
> insights essential for security analysts. Moreover, they typically fail to cover the
> complete three-stage workflow. To address these issues, we introduce CyberThreat-Eval,
> which is collected from the daily CTI workflow of a world-leading company. This
> expert-annotated benchmark assesses LLMs on practical tasks across all three stages
> as mentioned above. It utilizes analyst-centric metrics that measure factual accuracy,
> content quality, and operational costs. Our evaluation using this benchmark reveals
> important insights into the limitations of current LLMs. For example, LLMs often lack
> the nuanced expertise required to handle complex details and struggle to distinguish
> between correct and incorrect information. To address these challenges, the CTI
> workflow incorporates both external ground-truth databases and human expert knowledge.
> TRA allows human experts to iteratively provide feedback for continuous improvement.
> The code is available at GitHub and HuggingFace."

## Limitations of this source

- **Precision-only for IoC extraction.** No recall and no F1 are reported for the IoC
  task, and the paper does not say why. src-0003's headline figure is an F1. The two
  numbers are therefore NOT directly comparable, and any comparison must say so. A
  precision-only figure is silent about missed indicators, which is exactly the axis on
  which legacy tools were weakest in src-0003.
- **Different corpus, different system.** Table 4 is explicitly headed "Vanilla LLMs" —
  it measures an LLM prompted to extract, not a regex-prefiltered, human-validated
  pipeline like LANCE. It is a measurement of a different system on a different corpus,
  not a replication attempt.
- Corpus provenance is "a world-leading company" and is not named; the benchmark's
  difficulty relative to PRISM's 50 public threat reports cannot be assessed from the
  paper.
- Author affiliation is Microsoft/Microsoft Research plus HKUST. The models evaluated
  are all OpenAI models (GPT-4o, o3-mini, and fine-tunes), i.e. models served by that
  organisation's own cloud partner. No non-OpenAI model is in Table 4. This is a
  vendor-adjacent model selection and should be read the same way src-0005's
  CrowdStrike/Meta affiliation is.
- Retrieved via automated page fetch of the arXiv HTML; Table 4 was pulled in full and
  cross-checked against the body-text sentence quoting "around 0.82-0.85", which agrees.
  Figures beyond Table 4 and the abstract were not verified in this pass.
