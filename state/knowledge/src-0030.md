## Summary

The paper argues that LLM outputs vary across repeated identical requests, enumerates
four distinct mechanisms for that variation, shows that setting temperature to zero
removes only one of them for API-served models, demonstrates the downstream consequence
on regression significance in an economics application, and proposes a reporting
standard for articles and replication packages.

## Abstract, verbatim and complete

"Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to generate data for research.
Typical use cases are classifications, annotations, information extraction, and
generation of numerical scores. Unlike conventional measurements, LLM outputs can vary
across repeated requests even when the prompt and apparent model settings remain
unchanged. This variation arises from deliberate sampling, silent model updates,
numerical rounding, or expert routing. Setting a dedicated temperature parameter to zero
removes deliberate sampling when that option is available, but it does not eliminate the
other sources of randomness. Exact reproduction is therefore generally not possible when
using proprietary application programming interfaces. Local execution of open-weight
models offers greater control, but reproducibility still depends on the complete hardware
and software stack. We illustrate these issues through sentiment classifications of
corporate filings and examine their consequences for downstream regression results. We
then propose a reporting standard for articles and replication packages, as well as
guidance for data editors and authors. Together, these findings and recommendations
establish that LLM outputs should be treated as draws from a distribution rather than as
fixed measurements."

## Key claims (verbatim-supported)

1. **Four named mechanisms produce run-to-run variation, and temperature zero removes
   only one of them.** Section 3 headings, verbatim: "3.1 Deliberate sampling", "3.2
   Silent model updates", "3.3 Floating point rounding", "3.4 Expert routing". Verbatim:
   "Setting T=0 removes deliberate sampling but...does not eliminate all sources of
   randomness in frontier models served through online APIs." Abstract, verbatim: "Exact
   reproduction is therefore generally not possible when using proprietary application
   programming interfaces."

2. **Determinism at T=0 is achievable locally but not through an API, demonstrated on
   this paper's own runs.** Section 4.2, Figure 3, verbatim: for a locally deployed model,
   "the distribution of statistics based on Gemma-4 deployed locally...is entirely
   concentrated at a single value under T=0"; for an API-served model at T=0, "the
   distribution becomes much more concentrated but does not collapse to a single value.
   Instead, the estimates take only four distinct values (with one occurring only
   rarely)."

3. **The headline normative claim.** Abstract, verbatim: "LLM outputs should be treated as
   draws from a distribution rather than as fixed measurements." Its practical corollary,
   Section 4/recommendations, verbatim: "Repeated calls on a documented random subsample
   can provide an affordable assessment."

## Further quotes with locations

- Section 4.2, Figure 2, on the downstream cost of ignoring run-to-run variation in a
  GPT-5.6 sentiment classification: "around 5% of the t-statistics fall to the left of
  the 90% confidence significance threshold. Hence, a researcher may in around 5% of the
  cases report a statistically significant result, whereas in fact the related statistic
  passes the threshold only by luck."
- On silent model updates: "A provider may alter the model behind an unchanged public
  name...Chen et al. (2024) document substantial variation in the behavior of GPT-4 over
  time. In one simple mathematical task involving the classification of numbers as prime
  or composite, reported accuracy fell from 84 percent to 51 percent within three
  months."
- On post-publication reproducibility: "A model may change during data collection, so
  observations processed on different dates may not be comparable. It may also change
  after publication, preventing reproducibility verifiers from recovering the original
  outputs." And: "Though providers typically pin specific legacy models through their
  APIs, a proprietary model version may disappear completely."
