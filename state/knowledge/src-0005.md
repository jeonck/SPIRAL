## Summary

CyberSOCEval (arXiv 2509.20166) is an industry-originated benchmark (large author list
including senior authors from CrowdStrike, e.g. Sven Krasser, and Meta, e.g. Joshua
Saxe), released as part of Meta's open-source "CyberSecEval 4" suite. It targets two
SOC-defender-relevant domains the authors say are under-covered by existing
benchmarks: malware analysis and threat-intelligence reasoning. Unlike src-0002
(CTIBench, mid-2024 models), this benchmark evaluates newer/larger LLMs including
reasoning models with test-time scaling, giving a more current (2025-era) data point on
whether model scaling is closing the CTI reliability gap.

## Key claims

1. Larger/more modern LLMs perform better on CyberSOCEval's malware-analysis and
   threat-intelligence-reasoning tasks, "confirming the training scaling laws
   paradigm" — i.e. raw scale continues to help on these tasks as of the 2025 model
   generation tested.
2. Reasoning models using test-time scaling do NOT get the same performance boost on
   these CTI/malware tasks that they get on coding and math benchmarks — suggesting
   generic "reasoning" post-training does not transfer well to cybersecurity threat
   analysis, and that domain-specific training/data is likely still necessary.
3. "Current LLMs are far from saturating our evaluations," i.e. as of this benchmark's
   publication, no evaluated model is near a ceiling — there remains substantial
   measured headroom/unreliability in both malware analysis and threat-intel reasoning
   tasks industry-wide, not just in academic benchmarks like CTIBench.

## Relevant quotes

- "core defensive domains with inadequate coverage in current benchmarks" (stated
  motivation for building CyberSOCEval).
- "larger, more modern LLMs tend to perform better, confirming the training scaling
  laws paradigm."
- "reasoning models leveraging test time scaling do not achieve the same boost as in
  coding and math."
- "Current LLMs are far from saturating our evaluations."

## Limitations of this source

- Retrieved via automated abstract-page summarization; exact model names and
  per-model/per-task numeric scores were not captured in this pass and should be
  pulled from the full paper before citing specific figures.
- Industry-affiliated authorship (CrowdStrike, Meta) — the benchmark's task design and
  framing may reflect these organizations' commercial priorities (e.g. SOC tooling
  relevant to CrowdStrike's product line); treat comparative claims about specific
  vendors' models with that in mind.
- As with CTIBench (src-0002), this is a benchmark snapshot tied to the specific model
  set evaluated at publication time (2025); results will age as new model generations
  release.

### Task format, construction and metric — never previously recorded, and material

- Both benchmarks are **multi-select multiple choice**, not free-form extraction or
  generation. Metric verbatim: "Evaluation is based on accuracy: the share of questions
  for which the system selects all correct options and only the correct options."
- 609 malware-analysis Q&A test cases from detonated public Windows malware samples.
- 588 threat-intelligence-reasoning Q&A pairs from 45 distinct threat-intelligence
  reports; the report is supplied to the model "via a set of images (one per report
  page)".
- Questions were **LLM-generated then human-validated**: Llama 3.2 90B (malware
  analysis); "We used Llama 4 Maverick and Llama 3.2 90B with two strategies for
  synthetically generating questions" — Category-Based and Relationship-Based —
  "which we then sampled from and manually validated" (threat intelligence).

Consequence for cross-source comparison: src-0005's percentages are **not commensurable**
with src-0002's CTI-ATE F1 or src-0007's precision/recall, which score free-form output.
This is the same task-format non-commensurability cycle 22 established for src-0003's
97.6% classification score, and it is now the third instance in this base.

### The paper's own stated limitations, verbatim — none of them were in this base

- "We acknowledge that the use of multiple choice format in our evaluations does not
  provide a perfect proxy for capabilities"
- "It is important to also recognize the existence of performance bias that may be
  introduced in cases where the model under test is the same, or has similarities with
  the set of models that were used in synthetic data generation pipelines"
- "Another important limitation to call out is that the contextual data provided to an AI
  system under test in each of our benchmarks may differ significantly from the
  contextual data that a given business or security operations center observes in
  production."

The second is a live contamination concern, because the question generators are Llama
models and Llama models are among those under test.

### Why this matters and is not bookkeeping

Jaccard over sets and F1 over sets are monotone transforms of one another (J = F1/(2-F1)),
so a per-model Jaccard is of the SAME FAMILY as the set-overlap F1 scores this project
compares across studies. The base's standing claim that src-0005 is one of "SEVEN
documented non-commensurable instances" rests, in the graph's own words, on "src-0005's
all-or-nothing multi-select" — and that stated GROUND is now known to be incomplete.

The CONCLUSION may well survive on a DIFFERENT and better ground, which is recorded here
so no successor over-reads this: src-0005's Jaccard is computed over a CLOSED, SMALL
multiple-choice option set, whereas src-0002's CTI-ATE F1 and src-0007's precision/recall
are computed over OPEN free-form extraction. That is a real non-commensurability and it is
NOT the one the state asserts. A successor must repair the reason, not merely keep the
verdict.
